# Video script (5 minutes max)

Read at a calm pace (~130–150 words/min); total ≈ 620 words ≈ 4:10–4:50. The same text is in each slide's speaker notes, so PowerPoint's *Record Slide Show* shows it while you record.

Recording tips: PowerPoint → Slide Show → Record → export as MP4 (1080p). Keep the cover on screen while introducing the team. Point with the cursor at the bubble/bar you name. Upload to YouTube as *Unlisted* or to Google Drive with link sharing on, then paste the link in the form.

## COVER (~20 s)

Hi, we are [team]. Our project is called Already Baked In. Using the UN World Population Prospects 2024, we split every country's population change between 2024 and 2100 into four exact factors: the momentum of today's age structure, falling mortality, fertility, and migration. The line on this slide is the whole world in one equation: 8.13 billion people today, times those four factors, gives 10.17 billion by 2100.

## SLIDE 1 (~55 s)

Most population stories start and end with the fertility rate. This chart shows why that is not enough. Each bubble is a country: across is its 2024 fertility rate, up is its projected change to 2100. Fertility clearly matters, but look left of the replacement line at 2.1. 131 countries are already below replacement, yet 51 of them, home to 2.6 billion people, still have age structures that push growth, and 26 will still be larger in 2100. First we cleaned the data: the raw file mixes countries with 318 regional and income aggregates, so we kept the 237 countries and checked that they add up exactly to the UN world total. Our question: how do the four forces combine, country by country?

## SLIDE 2 (~70 s)

How do we separate the forces? The UN publishes alternative runs of its projection, and four of them form a chain. The momentum run fixes fertility at replacement, freezes mortality and removes migration, so it shows what today's age structure alone would do. Adding the projected fall in mortality, then projected fertility, then migration, we arrive at the official medium projection. Each step is a ratio, so the four factors multiply exactly to the total. For the world, age structure alone takes us from 8.1 to 9.6 billion, longer lives add more, and fertility, at ×0.95, already pulls the total down. The surprise is on the right: if every woman had exactly 2.1 children starting today, the world would reach 10.7 billion, more than the UN's own path. This century's growth is already baked in.

## SLIDE 3 (~65 s)

Here is the same split for each region and for large countries. Segments to the right push population up, to the left pull it down, and the diamond is the net result. Africa more than doubles because a young population and above-replacement fertility compound. Europe shrinks by 21%, and migration is the main force holding it up; without it the change would be −40%. India and Canada both have below-replacement fertility, yet India grows on momentum and Canada on migration. Türkiye shrinks by about a quarter: low fertility and projected emigration outweigh its young age structure. Across all countries, fertility explains 59% of the differences in growth, momentum 32% and migration 9%, so no single indicator is enough. A second chain of UN runs gives the same main driver for 97% of countries.

## SLIDE 4 (~60 s)

How sure can we be? For the median country, the UN's 95% range for 2100 spans a factor of about 6. That width depends mostly on size, not on which engine drives growth: small, migration-dependent places like Singapore and Qatar are the most uncertain, and for three places the lower bound is exactly zero, a model artefact we flag. Following the ASA ethical guidelines, we treat projections as scenarios and show their uncertainty. The zero-migration and replacement-fertility runs are accounting tools, not policy targets: decisions about children belong to people. We describe migration without crisis language. We report both country counts and population weights, because weighting changes the answer. And our code is open, so anyone can reproduce every number. Our takeaway: most of this century's growth is baked in by today's age structure, and fertility, momentum and migration must be read together. Thank you.
