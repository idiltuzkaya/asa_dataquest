# Video script — English

Six pages: cover, four content pages, references. About 579 words. Record below five minutes. Figures refer to 1 January unless stated. Team introduction is to be added using actual member information.

## Page 1

Our question is why populations can keep growing even when fertility is low. We use the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024, covering 237 countries and areas. We focus on 2050, compare longer horizons, and ask how much confidence we should place in the direction of projected change.

## Page 2

The horizontal axis shows births per woman in 2023. The vertical axis shows population change from January 2024 to January 2050 under the United Nations Medium projection. We use 2.1 births per woman as an approximate threshold for low fertility. Of 130 countries and areas below the fertility threshold, 64 still grow. These are country counts, so a small territory and a large country each count once. The growing group contains about 58 percent of the population of all low fertility places. Low fertility therefore does not mean immediate decline. But the projected direction is uncertain. Only six of those 64 have a 95 percent prediction interval entirely above their starting population. We will return to that distinction.

## Page 3

To examine the mechanisms, we compare a sequence of United Nations scenarios. The momentum scenario combines replacement fertility, constant mortality and zero net migration. Its change from the baseline measures the finite horizon effect of the starting age structure under those assumptions. We then allow projected mortality, projected fertility and finally projected migration. Differences between the successive population stocks give four contributions that add to the Medium population change. The bars show each contribution as a percentage of the starting population. Positive and negative components can cancel. India, China and Turkey illustrate different combinations. Replacement fertility here means a net reproduction rate of one. It does not impose exactly 2.1 births per woman everywhere. These are conditional model comparisons, not estimates of what a government policy would cause.

## Page 4

Turkey makes the time horizon and uncertainty especially clear. The January 2024 baseline is 87.37 million. By January 2050, the Medium projection reaches 91.31 million, an increase of 3.94 million. Our scenario decomposition attributes positive contributions of 13.38 million to momentum and 2.81 million to mortality changes. Fertility and migration contribute negative 7.63 and 4.62 million along this particular path. The United Nations 95 percent population interval for 2050 ranges from 74.57 to 107.92 million. It includes values below and above the starting population. By January 2100, the Medium projection declines to 65.67 million. Positive momentum is therefore compatible with eventual decline. These values belong to the 2024 revision and should not be presented as current population observations.

## Page 5

Our main pattern survives several checks, while exact counts depend on the definition. Among low fertility places with at least one million people, 39 of 81 grow by 2050. Using a net reproduction rate below one gives 66 out of 130. Extending the original cohort to 2100 reduces the growing count to 28. We also swap the fertility and migration order along a valid alternative scenario path. Their separate contributions change, so no component should be treated as uniquely determined. Of the 64 Medium growth cases, 58 have prediction intervals spanning the baseline. Country intervals are marginal and cannot provide a simultaneous interval for this count. We present migration and reproductive choices descriptively, respect the people represented, and disclose model assumptions. Future population is conditional, even when the arithmetic is exact.
